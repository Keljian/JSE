# JSE update - flags, warm contacts, and a big tidy up

Decent sized update this time. Four changes to how the app behaves, plus a fair
bit of cleanup underneath.

## Flags on jobs

Every scoring stage in JSE was built to answer "how well does this match". Ask a
language model that and it will always find you something. Point it at a clinical
services manager role and it won't tell you you're not a clinician. It finds an
angle. Stakeholder management, systems thinking, transferable leadership.

So JSE now puts flags on roles. A flag is one specific thing about a job you'd
want to know before you spend an evening on it. There are five kinds:

- **Credential gate.** The ad says you must hold something you don't have. A
  registration, a licence, a clearance, a finished degree.
- **Domain mismatch.** The day to day work is in a profession you don't practise.
- **Below your level.** The role is scoped or priced under your ceiling, which is
  where overqualification screening quietly bins you.
- **Above your level.** Needs a level your history can't evidence.
- **Evidence gap.** Something concrete the ad asks for that your resume doesn't
  show. A named platform, a team size, years in a discipline.

Every flag has to name the ad's own requirement and say why your resume doesn't
meet it. If it can't do both, it gets dropped before you see it. That rule
matters more than it sounds. Vague flags are noise, and once you've skimmed past
three useless ones you stop reading the useful fourth.

Flags don't do anything. They don't block you, they don't move your score, they
don't hide the job. They sit next to the score and you decide.

The first version of this was worse and I'll say so. I built it as a gate. It
returned a verdict of skip, stretch or clear, and on a skip it refused to
generate your documents unless you explicitly overrode it. It worked fine.
Using it felt awful. A language model was sitting between me and a decision about
my own career, and being overruled by one is worse than getting no help at all.
So I pulled it out and kept the detection without the authority.

You can dismiss any flag you disagree with, and add your own. Yours stay put when
the job gets re-analysed, because JSE can't work out for itself that the recruiter
wouldn't name the client. Only you know that.

There's a useful side effect to flags not gating anything. If nothing depends on
them, there's no reason to spend a separate AI call working them out, so they now
ride along with the triage pass that was already running. Same number of calls as
before. Triage also reads the whole ad now instead of the first 3,500 characters,
so a registration buried at the bottom of a long ad doesn't get missed.

## Who you know beats how well you match

Applications through a warm channel convert at a completely different rate to
cold portal submissions. Everyone knows this. Almost no tooling does anything
with it.

JSE was already recording the channel, but only on the outcome snapshot after the
application had gone out. Bit late to be useful.

Channel now sits on the job itself and it changes the ranking. A 70 scoring role
where you know someone comes above an 85 scoring cold job board submission in the
campaign plan. That inversion is deliberate. Score still orders things inside a
warmth tier, but warmth goes first, because a cold application gets compared side
by side against a better matched candidate and a warm one often doesn't. That gap
is worth more than fifteen points of keyword overlap.

On the pipeline board, warmth sits above the score but below your own priorities
and due dates. An overdue action still leads, as it should.

JSE also checks each job's employer against your contact book and puts "Possible
warm path: Dana Lee (COO)" on the card before you've built anything. It checks
the real employer first, so a recruiter listed role still finds your contact at
the end client.

There's a new dashboard nudge too. The old one asked whether you'd done any
hidden market work this week. This one asks a sharper question: of the
applications you actually sent, how many went out cold. You can have a busy week
of research and still fire everything into portals. When the mix skews cold it
says so, and names the live roles that already have a contact behind them.

## One file, the whole shortlist

My actual daily loop is that JSE sweeps overnight, then the survivors get a
proper go or no go and positioning pass. That handoff was manual copy and paste,
role by role, and it was easily the most annoying part of using my own tool.

There's now an Export triage packet button. One markdown or JSON file per sweep
with every surviving role, containing:

- the full ad text and any attached position description
- employer, salary, location, closing date, source
- match, fragment and composite scores, plus the stored analysis
- any flags, with the requirement behind each one
- warm path hits

Warm roles lead the file. Flagged roles stay in it, because pulling them out
would be making the same call the packet exists to help you make. You can exclude
specific flag types if you want it narrowed. The output folder is configurable,
so it can be a watched folder.

## Two document tracks, picked automatically

Overqualification screening is a real cause of rejections on coordinator and
support grade roles, and the fix has always been a stripped back CV. I was doing
that by hand when I remembered, which wasn't often.

JSE now sorts each role into full senior or stripped back and writes to a
different brief. It doesn't need another AI call to work that out. Triage already
reported the seniority direction, and the title and salary bands are already
parsed. Triage's read is decisive on its own, and the keyword rules need two
signals agreeing, so "IT Support Manager" doesn't get your resume stripped just
for containing the word support. You can pin the track by hand and it stays
pinned.

On the stripped track, the resume is written to the ad's actual scope. Same real
employers, same titles, same dates. Emphasis and depth change, facts never do. It
leads with hands on delivery of the ad's duties instead of budget ownership and
headcount, and drops senior scope bullets that don't map to the role.

The cover letter change matters more. It's told to name the seniority difference
in one honest sentence, early, and say plainly why this role and this employer
now. Otherwise the screener fills that gap in themselves, and they usually fill
it in unfavourably.

## Under the hood

Less interesting, all overdue.

**CI runs the tests now.** It was building installers on three platforms without
ever running the test suite. There's a fast gate in front of the builds now:
tests, Python lint, renderer lint, renderer build.

**Linting kept narrow on purpose.** Real defects only, so undefined names, unused
variables, mutable default arguments, bare excepts and React hook ordering. No
style rules. I left the React Compiler rules off after they flagged about twenty
optimisation suggestions and zero actual bugs. A linter that's red on day one is
a linter everyone learns to ignore. The first clean run turned up a dead handler
and a few other bits of rot.

**Installers stopped shipping my personal data.** The packaging config was
bundling my own search terms and my local council and university scrapers, files
that are gitignored precisely because they're personal. A build on my machine and
a build from a clean checkout produced two different apps from the same commit.
Neutral first run content now lives in a committed folder, and that's the only
thing packaged. There's a test holding it that way, since this sort of bug is
invisible in a diff.

**Python dependencies fully pinned**, transitives included. Before this, a
library could publish a release and change what an installer shipped with no diff
anywhere in the repo.

**Four big files split up.** The database layer was 9,191 lines in one file, the
renderer 6,072, the LLM layer 3,644, the bridge 3,509. All four are layered
packages now, with the entry points left where they were, so nothing that used
them had to change. Biggest remaining file is about 2,300 lines.

That one had a nasty surprise in it. Splitting a big module quietly breaks two
things, and both fail silently rather than loudly. Test monkeypatching stops
reaching the real code, so tests keep passing while quietly making live network
calls. My suite went from 4 seconds to 42 and I nearly missed why. The database
path variable also gets a second copy, which means tests can look isolated while
writing to your real database. Both are handled, both have tests holding them in
place, and there's a long comment explaining it for whoever hits it next.
Probably me.

**Added an error boundary**, so a rendering bug gives you a message and a reload
button instead of a white window. Desktop app, no address bar, nowhere to go.

## Testing

209 tests passing now, up from 89 when I started this batch. The one I'd point at
is the check that a role with flags still goes all the way through full analysis
with those flags fed into the prompt. Without it, the test proving flags get
recorded would pass even if the rest of the pipeline was broken.
