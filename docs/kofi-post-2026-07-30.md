# JSE update: teaching it to say no

Most job-search tooling has the same bug, and JSE had it too. Every scoring
stage is built to answer *how well does this match* — and a model asked that
question will always find something. Point it at a clinical services manager
role and it won't say "you're not a clinician." It'll find an angle. Stakeholder
management. Systems thinking. Transferable leadership.

That's not analysis. That's a machine that has been asked to be helpful,
producing an application you were never going to win.

This update is mostly about fixing that, plus three other things that came out
of actually looking at what converts.

---

## 1. A gate that's allowed to say "don't bother"

There's a new stage between the cheap first-pass triage and the expensive full
analysis. It doesn't score anything. It answers one question: **is there a
reason not to apply at all?**

It checks three things and nothing else:

- **An eligibility gate you don't meet** — a mandatory registration, licence,
  clearance, citizenship requirement, or completed degree.
- **A core domain mismatch** — the actual day-to-day work sits in a profession
  you don't practise. Sharing an industry or a word in the title isn't overlap.
- **Seniority misalignment, in either direction** — the role is scoped under
  your ceiling (so you get screened out as overqualified) or above what your
  history can evidence.

Everything else — missing tools, a platform you haven't touched, a smaller team
than they want — is a *gap*, not a blocker. Gaps don't stop an application.

It returns one of three verdicts: **skip**, **stretch** (with the specific gaps
named), or **clear**.

Three details that took the most thought:

**The prompt says "no" is a good answer.** In as many words. It tells the model
that reframing a mismatch as an angle is a failure of the stage, not a service.
The whole reason the previous chain never returned a decisive negative is that
nothing ever told it one was wanted.

**It reads the ad's own words first, deterministically.** Before the model sees
anything, plain code extracts the "must have / essential / mandatory" sentences
out of the ad and flags the ones naming a credential. The model then judges that
short list instead of re-reading 900 words of company mission statement, where
the narrative pulls it back toward finding an angle. Anything phrased as
*preferred*, *desirable*, or *highly regarded* can never be a blocker.

**It's guarded against becoming the opposite problem.** A gate that over-fires
is just as bad as one that never fires — it silently bins roles you'd have got.
So: a skip must name the ad's requirement *and* why your resume can't meet it,
or it's downgraded to a stretch. A low-confidence skip is downgraded too. A
failed or unparseable gate call returns no opinion at all and the job carries on
to full analysis. It can only ever be as decisive as its evidence.

When it does say skip, that actually stops things: full analysis never runs, and
document generation is blocked in the workspace, the campaign plan, and the
batch. You can override it — one click, and the override is recorded against the
job so you can see later what you overruled and how it went.

When it says stretch, the named gaps go into the full-analysis prompt with an
instruction to answer them directly and let the unanswerable ones lower the
score, rather than paper over them.

## 2. Who you know now outranks how well you match

The uncomfortable thing in my own numbers: applications through a warm channel
convert at a completely different rate to cold portal submissions. Everyone
knows this. Almost no tooling acts on it.

JSE already recorded the channel — but only on the outcome snapshot, *after* the
application went out. Which is exactly one step too late to be useful.

Channel now lives on the job itself, and it ranks. A **70-scoring role where you
know someone** now sits above an **85-scoring cold job-board submission** in the
campaign plan. That's a deliberate inversion: the score orders things within a
warmth tier, but warmth comes first. A cold application is judged side by side
against a more directly matched candidate. A warm one often isn't. That
difference is bigger than fifteen points of keyword overlap.

On the pipeline board warmth sits above the score but below your own priorities
and due dates — an overdue action still leads, because it should.

JSE also cross-references every job's employer against your contact book and
flags **"Possible warm path: Dana Lee (COO)"** right on the card, before you've
built anything. It checks the real employer first, so a recruiter-listed role
still finds the contact at the end client.

And there's a new dashboard nudge that's sharper than the old one. The existing
nudge asked whether you'd done *any* warm-channel work this week. This one asks
a different question: **of the applications you actually sent, how many went out
cold?** You can have a busy week of hidden-market research and still fire
everything into portals. When the mix skews cold, it says so — and names the
live roles that already have a contact sitting behind them.

## 3. One file, the whole shortlist

My real daily loop: JSE sweeps overnight, then the survivors get a proper
go/no-go and positioning pass. That handoff was manual copy-paste, role by role,
and it was the single most annoying part of using my own tool.

New **Export triage packet** button. One markdown and/or JSON file per sweep,
containing every surviving role with:

- the full ad text and any attached position description
- extracted metadata — employer, salary, location, closing date, source
- match, fragment and composite scores, plus the stored analysis
- the blocker-gate verdict with its hard blockers and named gaps
- any warm-path hits

Warm roles lead the file. Roles the gate already ruled out are left out, because
handing them back would be handing back a decision that's already been made. The
output folder is configurable, so it can be a watched folder.

## 4. Two document tracks, chosen automatically

Overqualification screening is a real rejection cause on coordinator and
support-grade roles, and the fix has always been a stripped-back CV. I was doing
that by hand, when I remembered.

JSE now classifies each role as **full senior** or **stripped back** and writes
the documents to a different brief. It doesn't need another AI call to decide —
the blocker gate already judged the level, and the title and salary bands are
already parsed. The gate's judgement is decisive on its own because it read the
whole ad against your whole resume. The keyword heuristics need two agreeing
signals, so "IT Support Manager" doesn't get your resume stripped just for
containing the word *support*. You can pin the track manually and it sticks.

On the stripped track the resume is written to the ad's actual scope. Same real
employers, same titles, same dates — **emphasis and depth change, facts never
do.** It leads with hands-on delivery of the ad's duties instead of budget
ownership and headcount, and drops senior-scope bullets that don't map.

The cover letter change matters more. It's instructed to name the seniority
difference in one honest sentence, early, and say plainly why this role and this
employer now — rather than pretending the gap isn't there and leaving the
screener to fill in the reason themselves. They will fill it in. Usually
unfavourably.

## Under the hood

Less exciting, all overdue:

- **CI now actually runs the tests.** It was building installers on three
  platforms without ever running the test suite. There's now a fast gate —
  tests, Python lint, renderer lint, renderer build — that all three platform
  builds depend on.
- **Linting, deliberately narrow.** Real defect classes only: undefined names,
  unused variables, mutable default arguments, bare excepts, React hook ordering.
  No style rules. The React Compiler rules were left off on purpose — they
  flagged about twenty optimisation notes and zero defects, and a linter that's
  red on day one is a linter everyone learns to ignore. The first clean run found
  a dead handler, an unused log callback, and a few other bits of rot.
- **Installers stopped shipping my personal data.** The packaging config was
  bundling my own search terms and my local council/university scrapers — files
  that are gitignored precisely because they're personal. A build on my machine
  and a build from a clean checkout produced two different applications from the
  same commit. Neutral first-run content now lives in a committed `defaults/`
  folder, and that's the only thing packaged. There's a test asserting it, since
  this kind of bug is invisible in a diff.
- **Python dependencies are fully pinned**, transitives included. Previously a
  library could publish a release and change what an installer shipped with no
  diff anywhere in the repo.
- **Archived a stale database** sitting at the repo root next to the live one.
  Nothing had gone wrong yet. It was going to.

## Testing

Everything above is covered by tests rather than by hope — 162 passing, including
the gate's downgrade rules, the warmth ranking inversion, the packet contents,
the two-track selection, and the packaging guard. The one I like most asserts
that a *stretch* verdict still reaches full analysis with its gaps injected —
without it, the test proving *skip* short-circuits would pass even if the whole
pipeline were broken.
